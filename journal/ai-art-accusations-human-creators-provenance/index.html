The internet is calling real art AI. A detector will not fix that.

An artist can show sketches, layers and a timelapse and still hear the accusation. The answer is not another confident guess. It is a better record of how work was made.

THE SHORT ANSWER

You cannot reliably decide whether a work is AI-generated from how polished, smooth or unusual it looks. Ask for source files, process stages, rights information and, where supported, verifiable Content Credentials. None is a universal test on its own. For businesses commissioning artwork, define acceptable AI assistance and attribution before production starts.

SamDoesArts published Real Artists are Being Accused of AI Art now.. in October 2026. The title names a problem digital artists know increasingly well: viewers are treating stylistic clues as verdicts about authorship. We verified the video's publication and creator description, but did not obtain a full reliable transcript. The broader phenomenon is documented by artists themselves.

One unusually clear first-person account comes from illustrator David Revoy. In August 2026 he collected repeated online accusations that his hand-drawn art and comics were generated, despite his publicly shared process and timelapses. In subsequent production notes, he described how changes in his brushwork or rendering style were themselves treated as proof. That should make us pause before assuming that a detailed painting, a strange hand or a smooth gradient tells us how the work was made.

Three things people confuse when they say “AI art”

QuestionWhat it meansEvidence to request
Who made the expressive choices?Human authorship and editorial control.Sketches, source layers, iteration notes, art direction, revisions.
Were AI tools involved?Some assistance may have been used for ideation, masking or generation.Creator disclosure and tool/process record. Degree of assistance matters.
Can the work be used commercially?Rights, licenses and permission for the intended purpose.Written license, source ownership, asset/stock provenance and consent.

These questions overlap, but they are not interchangeable. A human can produce an image with digital tools. A generative model can create an image without full commercial clearance for every element. A legally licensed piece can still have weak documentation. The US Copyright Office's January 2025 report also distinguishes human expressive choices from merely entering prompts when analyzing copyrightability under US law. That framework is jurisdiction-specific, not a worldwide verdict.

Why an AI detector cannot serve as the jury

People notice familiar patterns: peculiar fingers, very glossy skin, inconsistent backgrounds, repeated textures. Some generated work shows these artifacts. Some human work uses deliberate abstraction, blended media, digital brushes, unusual anatomy or stylized color. A surface resemblance is a reason to ask a question, not a reason to publicly accuse someone of fraud.

Likewise, detector scores and “this looks like AI” comments are not a defensible standalone authentication method. They can be wrong, and a confident false claim can damage an artist’s reputation. Where provenance really matters, start with the original project and its history rather than a screenshot circulated through social media.

What Content Credentials actually add

The C2PA standard and Content Authenticity Initiative guidance describe cryptographically signed records that can carry information about an image's origin and editing history. When properly created and retained, these are useful affirmative evidence. They are not an oracle that labels every image true or false.

Missing provenance is missing information. It is not proof of AI generation.

A platform may strip metadata. A screenshot can omit the original Content Credentials. A work can have a complete documented human process without embedded C2PA records. The Adobe-led open-source FAQ explicitly warns that Content Credentials alone do not answer whether an image is fake, and that absent credentials are not negative evidence of authenticity.

A practical commissioning checklist for brands and agencies

  1. Define the creative and AI policy in the brief. Decide what is acceptable: fully hand-made, AI-assisted editing, licensed generative assets, or a clearly labeled synthetic illustration. A blanket “no AI” policy may be too vague to enforce.
  2. Agree on ownership and use. Specify the source-file handover, commercial license, territories, platforms, duration, publicity and permitted modifications. Ask about stock, reference photographs, faces and third-party marks.
  3. Request a lightweight work-in-progress trail. Save two or three process checkpoints, useful source layers and key creative decisions. Do not demand sensitive personal files or excessive surveillance of the artist.
  4. Review claims proportionately. A small social illustration need not carry the same evidence burden as an expensive campaign, regulated claim or political image. But the final delivery should honestly state relevant synthetic elements.
  5. Record the disclosure. Where platforms or laws require synthetic-media labels or endorsements, include those in the publication checklist, not as an afterthought.
  6. Never turn a style critique into an accusation. Ask the creator privately about provenance and follow an appeal process if a policy is in dispute.

Frequently asked questions

Can ChatGPT or an AI detector tell me whether a picture was generated?

They can help describe visible features, but a guess based on pixels should not be presented as proof of origin. Ask for process evidence and check supported provenance records.

Does a missing C2PA label mean art was generated?

No. Many legitimate files lack signed metadata; screenshots, export workflows and platform transformations can remove it. Absence is not a verdict.

What should I ask a freelance illustrator or AI creative studio for?

A clear brief, agreed AI policy, documented references and licenses, meaningful checkpoints, the final deliverable and any contracted source assets. Match the request to project value and avoid intrusive demands.
