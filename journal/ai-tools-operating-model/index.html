AI tools aren't the operating model.

The model is the work around them: who makes the decision, which systems are allowed to talk, what happens when something breaks, and how anyone knows it worked.

Businesses have more ways than ever to connect AI to the software they already use. The technology can read a request, interpret a document, draft an update or call another application. But a connector does not tell the business who should approve the result. A prompt does not decide which customer record is authoritative. A successful demo does not prove a process will survive an outage.

That is the difference between owning an AI tool and having an AI operating model.

Start with a decision, not a product.

Take a familiar case: a request arrives in an inbox, somebody checks the customer's details, updates a spreadsheet, alerts a colleague and follows up two days later.

A tool-first conversation asks which chatbot or workflow builder can do all five steps. A useful operating-model conversation asks five different questions:

  1. Trigger: What exact event starts the process, and what should not?
  2. Source: Which system owns the customer record, and what makes the input trustworthy?
  3. Decision: What can be handled by a fixed rule, what needs judgment, and what must be reviewed by a person?
  4. Exception: Where does a duplicate, missing field, failed API call or ambiguous request go?
  5. Outcome: What observable change tells us the task is finished correctly?

Sometimes the resulting system needs an AI model. Sometimes it needs a form, a clean source of data and two well-designed rules.

The connector is a bridge. Permission is the architecture.

Microsoft's Power Platform can govern which connector groups are allowed to share data, using data loss prevention policies. Google's Apps Script can extend Sheets and connect services, but its triggers and services have authorization and quota limits. These are engineering constraints, not fine print. Microsoft documents the policy model, while Google documents Apps Script quotas and trigger behavior.

Before joining a CRM, inbox, document store and AI system, document which data may cross each connection, which credentials it uses, who owns those credentials and what happens when access expires. A workflow that only works while its creator is signed in is fragile by design.

Give the workflow a human-shaped escape route.

Reliable systems have a place for uncertainty. For example, an AI assistant might classify a request with reasonable confidence but encounter two possible customer records. It should not quietly pick one and continue. It should flag the ambiguity, retain context and ask the right owner to decide.

That principle is consistent with the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, which emphasizes governance, mapping, measurement and management. OpenAI also documents guardrails and human approval points for agent workflows with consequential actions.

Measure the boring things that actually matter.

Before building, record a modest baseline: how many requests arrive, how long handling takes, how often data must be corrected, and what proportion needs human review. After implementation, measure the same items. Count failed runs and rework. An impressive number of AI-generated drafts means little if a person must repair every one.

A practical first-week test

  1. Choose one recurring workflow, not the entire department.
  2. Observe five to ten recent examples, including the awkward ones.
  3. Draw the trigger, source of truth, decision, owner and failure path.
  4. Identify the smallest change that eliminates avoidable manual work.
  5. Run with a human check before increasing autonomy.

This is an assessment method, not a guarantee of time or cost savings. The appropriate design depends on the systems, data and risk involved.
