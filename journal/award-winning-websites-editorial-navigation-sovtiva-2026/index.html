What if a website were a place you wanted to wander?

From Pentagram Archive to Dropbox Brand and Igloo Inc, we studied recognized digital experiences and rebuilt Sovtiva's reading experience around quieter type, purposeful discovery and small interactions.

Sovtiva ResearchNovember 13, 2026Research + design decisions

The loudest website in the room is not always the one you remember. Overweight headlines, enormous transitions and an infinite sequence of cards can impress for ten seconds and then make it difficult to find a single useful answer. We wanted a different kind of ambition for Sovtiva: the texture of a well-edited magazine, the clarity of a good product, and a sense that the next click may reveal something worth discovering.

We started with a critique of our own site. Our shared article stylesheet used display headings above 800 weight and unusually tight negative tracking; the Journal index followed the same pattern. Those settings are forceful, but they made long research titles feel more like campaign billboards than editorial reading. We kept the recognizable navy, pale aqua, burgundy, olive and warmer paper. We changed the voice.

01

The winners we studied, and the limits of what an award proves

The Webby Awards named Pentagram Archive its 2026 Best User Experience winner; DICH™ Fashion won Best User Interface; Igloo Inc won Best Visual Design – Aesthetic; Docusign won Best Practices. The Renaissance Edition won Best Home Page, and Pinterest Predicts 2026 won the trend/insight reporting category. CSS Design Awards named Dropbox Brand its 2025 Website of the Year, with Exat Typeface winning Best UI in its specialist awards.

These are independently listed distinctions, not proof of any site's conversion rate, accessibility score, traffic or revenue. We used them as lenses for studying information design: does a site offer a distinct identity, an obvious way to explore, and a reason to return? We did not reuse their assets, copy their layouts, or claim our design now has an award.

02

The first change was subtraction: fewer typographic shouts

Our first revision replaced heavy research headlines with Instrument Serif. It made the interface quieter, but the letters still felt too tall and narrow across long titles. We went back to the type. The current edition uses Fraunces at a restrained medium weight for its wider, more grounded letterforms, paired with DM Sans for paragraphs and navigation. Headlines are smaller, spacing is less compressed and long research titles have room to breathe. The result is an editorial direction we can judge by readability rather than spectacle.

The change is not a blanket invitation to tiny gray type. We retained a comfortable reading width, restrained metadata, strong focus outlines and accessible body contrast. The serif is the invitation; the sans serif does the work. Both have system fallbacks so the page remains readable even if the external font never arrives.

03

A beautiful page is not yet an interesting place

An editorial index should let a reader follow a thread. On our Journal we added a small 'reading room': type a question to search titles and summaries, switch between existing editorial sections, choose cards or a compact reading list, or ask for a surprise article from the currently visible selection. None of these controls contacts a model or sends a query to an external search provider. It is a lightweight local way to find the work we have already published.

If JavaScript is disabled, the complete ordinary Journal remains visible. We show enhancement controls only when the finder is ready. Search results are announced to assistive technologies, controls have visible keyboard focus, and the page includes a no-results message. It is intentionally less dramatic than a full-screen canvas and more useful on a phone.

04

Make the homepage an invitation, not an obstacle course

Sovtiva's most important question remains 'What brings you here?' with its four existing routes to services, specialists, digital tools and our wider creative world. We did not turn that entry point into a maze. We introduced an optional editorial path nearby: three researched questions that open published pieces, plus an entrance into the broader Journal.

Each entry uses typography and a quiet color shift rather than stock photos or a copied character. On narrow screens it becomes a swipeable row with native horizontal scrolling. Nothing auto-advances or hides a buyer's original path to services.

A second look at interaction. The Symphony of Vines offers a different lesson: movement can respond to the visitor and belong to the story. For Sovtiva, a lighter version of that idea is more useful. Our homepage now offers a surprise editorial route while its original business-service paths remain obvious, without a large 3D dependency.

05

Interaction should make a choice easier

The point of motion is feedback: where is the reader, what can be selected, and what changed? This edition includes restrained hover and focus states, a reading-progress indicator in browsers supporting CSS scroll-driven animation, and a native, keyboard-accessible article finder. Each of these additions can disappear without destroying the article or the primary navigation.

We followed the W3C guidance for people who prefer reduced motion: non-essential transforms and the reading-progress animation are disabled when that preference is set. We also retained keyboard focus states and native links, and avoided adding large WebGL scenes or third-party animation libraries. 'Fun' should never mean motion someone cannot stop.

06

The benchmark is usefulness, not a design-awards imitation

Google's Interaction to Next Paint guidance identifies 200 milliseconds or less at the 75th percentile as a target for good response time. It does not mean Sovtiva currently meets that threshold. We have not measured our new release on representative phones, screen readers or real visitor traffic; CSS and source verification are not substitutes for user testing.

Our next quality gate is simple: verify that the four original homepage routes still work, the Journal finder can handle empty and non-empty queries, Spanish and English visitor paths remain understandable, and readers can navigate with a keyboard and reduced motion. Then review real engagement and service inquiry quality before adding more effects. A beautiful site that is harder to use would be a failed redesign.

07

What this means for future Sovtiva client work

The practical service opportunity is an editorial-experience audit: find the main question a customer is asking, reduce competing messages, design a navigable set of options, and demonstrate a real class of workflow rather than only promising 'AI'. It could become a repeatable website-design package, but this article does not claim a new commercial product, client success story or proven conversion uplift.

Across our own website, each new interaction will now need to earn its place: show a clear job, work without heavy dependencies, preserve accessibility, and supply a measurable next action. We would rather make ten thoughtful paths than build one expensive animation people abandon.
