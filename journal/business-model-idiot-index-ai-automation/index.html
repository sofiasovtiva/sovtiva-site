The Business-Model “Idiot Index”: What AI Can Change, and What It Cannot

Alex Hormozi argues that the same effort can produce radically different outcomes depending on the business vehicle. Useful idea. Dangerous if it becomes a promise that an “easy” industry prints money.

THE SHORT ANSWER

Choose a business where customer demand, contribution margin, distribution and delivery capability work together. Alex Hormozi’s 2027 “Idiot Index” is a useful provocation: some companies need enormous effort for little economic leverage, while others benefit from constraints that protect margins. AI can improve parts of execution. It cannot manufacture real demand, remove regulation or turn an unprofitable unit into a profitable business by itself.

In his November 2026 video How To Make More Money Than You Deserve (My 2027 Framework), Alex Hormozi makes a blunt point: businesses do not reward effort at a consistent rate. He groups models by how difficult they are to operate, how difficult they are to enter and how much opportunity they create once an operator has the capability to serve the market.

That is a provocative way to ask a legitimate strategic question. Where does the next hour of work produce the most enduring value? A founder who can barely keep the calendar, billing and delivery process running does not necessarily need another business idea. They might need to repair the operating model they already own.

These are interpretations of the creator's framework, not industry-level profit rankings. None of the categories promises a specific return. A regulated niche can have exceptional margins and still be inaccessible for a new entrant. An ordinary service business can produce excellent economics with disciplined operations and good customer relationships.

The calculation we would make before changing industries

Before leaving one venture for the latest supposedly lucrative niche, write down six numbers or assumptions you can verify:

  1. Demand: Who buys, how often and which specific problem makes the purchase necessary? Do not substitute search volume for purchase evidence.
  2. Acquisition: What does one qualified opportunity cost in time and money, and how frequently does it convert?
  3. Contribution: What remains after direct labor, vendors, payments, materials and rework?
  4. Delivery: Can the service meet its promise without every decision requiring the founder?
  5. Constraints: What licences, permissions, insurance, expertise, equipment or access must genuinely exist?
  6. Repeatability: Can a satisfied customer buy again, renew or refer someone without extraordinary founder intervention?

Then ask whether an improved operating system would unlock more value than a completely new venture. Switching industries carries learning costs that a catchy video cannot calculate for you.

Automation can improve response time and reduce repetitive work. But the economics depend on software costs, setup effort, oversight, failure recovery and how often the process runs. An hour saved on a low-frequency task may be less valuable than fixing the five inquiries per week that go unanswered.

A small implementation example

Imagine a specialist service company with strong demand but inconsistent follow-through. A prospect writes from the website, someone copies details into a spreadsheet, the owner misses the notification and the opportunity expires. The company does not need an “AI-native” rebrand. It needs an intake form, a clear CRM record, routing rules, a human-readable follow-up draft and an exception queue.

Questions founders ask before hiring help

What is the best business to start in 2027?

There is no answer independent of your access, expertise, demand and constraints. Evaluate actual buyer pain, delivery economics and licensing before copying a trend or a creator's example.

Can AI turn a service business into a scalable one?

It can standardize repetitive work and make information easier to act on. Scale still requires demand, accountable people, quality control and unit economics that remain healthy when volume increases.

Should I buy software or hire an AI automation consultant?

Buy a simple tool when a standard workflow already fits. Consider a scoped consultant or implementation partner when data, approvals and handoffs span multiple systems or when the team needs training to own the process.
