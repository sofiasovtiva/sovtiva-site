Sovtiva / Creative direction, 3D and evidence / 11.11.2026

We didn't need more effects.
We needed a director.

Four experiments, one uncomfortable correction and a more serious production plan: how to make cinematic 3D short videos with story, sound, and visual QA instead of another animated slide deck.

Sovtiva Research DeskFirst-party tests + external source researchV5: proposed, not rendered

We made a short video about a strange corner of the creator economy: 100,000 views could imply roughly $109 gross at an illustrative campaign rate, assuming those views qualify and the campaign actually pays. We tried static motion cards. Then aggressive kinetic typography. Then true 3D objects. Then a restrained, “premium” version. The more polished one wasn't necessarily the more interesting one.

That experience revealed a more useful question than “Which AI video generator is best?” How do we turn a strong visual idea into a directed scene that feels alive? The answer is a film-production process with software inside it, not software pretending to be the process.

The actual failure: 3D without filmmaking

Our earlier V3 was genuinely three-dimensional: the publicly versioned code uses VTK geometry, extruded type, spinning rings, lights and camera moves. But the scene was rendered at 540 × 960 pixels and upscaled to 1080 × 1920. V4 improved the palette and props, yet remained a stylized procedural render produced at 720 × 1280 before upscaling. Neither had a human voice, realistic motion or the editorial density of a directed short film. Those are statements about our own published source, not broad claims about VTK as a technology.

Better-looking materials alone would not fix it. There must be a reason to move the camera, a surprising change of perspective, a meaningful interaction between objects, an audio cue that changes the emotion, and a pay-off. A director would approve a storyboard before anyone renders hundreds of expensive frames.

Our next video should have one impossible, unforgettable visual transformation. Not fifteen unrelated effects.

A public case study worth examining, not copying

The open GitHub project ESCAPEMENT documents a 36-second Blender film made by an agent-driven Python workflow. Its repository includes a production bible, frame-accurate shot list, individual scene builders, a serialized GPU render queue, color/exposure tests, edit decisions and a licensed audio manifest. The author also describes crashes, hardware constraints and shots removed from the release. The work was built with Claude Code; Sovtiva does not use Claude, and this architecture does not require the same assistant.

The transferable lesson is not “one prompt made Hollywood.” It's separate direction, shot design, rendering, editing, sound and QA. A scripted shot is editable; the creative work remains iterative. We can adopt the system design while building original scenes and code.

What should V5 actually show?

We should keep V3's energy and build around a single absurd physical event. Imagine a phone in a dark, tactile studio. A view counter hits 100,000. The phone begins to shake; objects behind the glass gain physical weight. Brass tokens burst out into the room. The camera flies with one token until its engraved face catches the light: $109 gross*. That token becomes a printed campaign statement. A red approval stamp lands. Then the paper flips: not eligible, $0. The scene collapses into the original phone, ready to loop.

Voice draft: “¿Cien mil vistas? Suena a fortuna. Pero a una tarifa ilustrativa de 1.09 dólares por cada mil vistas válidas, son unos 109 dólares brutos. Si el video no califica, el pago puede ser cero. Vistas no son ganancias.” The rate is an illustrative campaign-offer calculation, not creator earnings, a typical payout or advice to buy views.

The cinematic standard is a coherent room, a real lens choice, tactile imperfect metal and paper, shadows moving over surfaces, motivated camera travel and precisely timed sound. Captions should be unobtrusive, never taking over the show.

How we'd produce and test it

  1. Director's brief. Lock the single idea, audience, Spanish script, visual tone and source citations. V3 = energy target; V4 = counterexample for pacing, not a template to keep polishing.
  2. Storyboard and animatic. Draw six or fewer key frames, record rough original voice, place temp effects, and build a low-resolution timing animatic. Refuse full rendering until the story works without beautiful textures.
  3. Assets with provenance. Build our own phone, coin and receipt models where practical; use documented CC0 HDRIs and textures where useful. Save origin, license and asset IDs outside the public repo if sensitive.
  4. Blender blocking and look development. 9:16 perspective camera, physically plausible object scale, key/fill/rim lighting, reflections, subtle depth of field and purposeful motion blur. Check stills at native 1080 × 1920 instead of assuming an upscale repairs poor source detail.
  5. Shot-specific renders. Render short handles around cuts. Use preview EEVEE quality, then higher-quality Cycles for a few hero moments if the hardware can bear it. Save frame sequences so failures don't restart the whole film.
  6. Edit, sound and finish. Cut to the original spoken line and music grid. Assemble in Resolve or the existing FFmpeg stack. Mix voice, foley, transitions and licensed music. No compulsory subscription.
  7. Real QA. Review on a phone with sound; check visual glitches, object collisions, clipped highlights, subtitle safe zones, end-frame continuity, source rights and the rate caveat.
  8. Audience learning. Only after approval and posting, compare hook variants with real platform retention, saves, replays, profile visits and qualified traffic. YouTube Shorts analytics explicitly distinguishes people who chose to watch from those who swiped away. No predicted viral score substitutes for that.

Music rights matter. TikTok recommends its Commercial Music Library for brand and commercial content. A TikTok-cleared track does not automatically grant a cross-platform soundtrack license. For reusable master files, use an original composition or a genuinely appropriate cross-platform license, with the exact license saved.

Do we need a connector, a GitHub app or a subscription?

No extra connector is required for the V5 core. Blender is a native program, not a ChatGPT plugin; our existing GitHub integration is enough to store code and production specs; and Descript/Adobe tools can support later editing as access permits. A GitHub App will not add GPU rendering. The actual gating resources are a verified Blender installation, workable local CPU/GPU performance and an approved media/storage workflow.

Optional later: vidIQ for analyzing existing TikTok/Shorts videos and patterns, or Runway/Higgsfield for a specific AI-generated scene that we can't produce practically in Blender. Those services require account connection and may use paid credits. None is essential to begin, and no purchase is justified before benchmarking the free pipeline.

For organizations using Remotion in a separate composition layer, its commercial license depends on company headcount and use case. Our first V5 does not need Remotion at all.

The acceptance test isn't “looks cool in a screenshot”

We should reject V5 if the first three seconds fail to establish the promise; if camera moves are arbitrary; if the metal looks like flat gradients; if reflections and shadows aren't coherent; if the story requires reading dense text; if the source footage or soundtrack lacks rights; if the $109 sounds guaranteed; or if the full MP4 fails human sound-on review. A pretty still is not an edited film.

What can we promise today? A researched production design, a documented source architecture, and an exact first test. We have not installed or benchmarked Blender on the target computer, rendered V5, booked a creator payout or proven a view uplift. Those results have to be earned and recorded.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a ChatGPT connector to use Blender?

No. Blender is desktop 3D software. ChatGPT can prepare Python scene scripts, while Blender actually renders them locally. External connectors are optional for particular third-party services.

Do we have to pay to get started?

No. Blender and the free DaVinci Resolve editor cover a first technical pilot. Paid generators, premium assets or remote render GPUs require separate cost approval.

What makes an AI video cinematic?

Intentional direction, camera movement, material detail, lighting, editing, sound design and story. Automating a render does not automatically deliver filmmaking.

Can GitHub Pages render the movie?

No. GitHub stores versioned source and can coordinate workflows, but GitHub Pages is a static hosting service, not a 3D render engine.
