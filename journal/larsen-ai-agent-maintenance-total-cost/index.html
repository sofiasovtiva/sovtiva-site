The AI agent is not finished when it works. Who will keep it working?

An AI agent that worked in a pilot and fails three months later is not a software success. The ongoing obligation is the product: permissions change, vendors update their APIs, inputs drift and real people need to know what happened.

A workflow can fail without going offline.

A server health check can say “green” while the assistant silently routes the wrong class of messages, reads an outdated policy or misses a duplicate. Useful monitoring must measure business outcomes: was a request processed correctly, did it reach the right record and could a person reconstruct the decision?

Larsen's AI automation services explicitly include shadow-mode testing, review queues and ongoing monitoring. Its public price list describes AI operations starting at $500 per month and reaching roughly $2,500 for broader support. These are vendor terms; current scope, response times and model costs require an actual quote.

Five operational commitments to put in writing.

  1. Ownership: a named responsible person for integrations, prompts, decision rules and exceptions.
  2. Observability: logs that show what triggered each action, what data it used and whether a human approved it.
  3. Evaluation: recurring samples against a labelled set of correct outcomes, including edge cases and permission failures.
  4. Recovery: ways to pause work, retry safely and roll back incorrect changes.
  5. Change management: versioned prompts, rule changes, API dependencies and documented release approvals.

Why 'human in the loop' must be specific.

A dashboard full of uncertain messages is not a workable supervision design. Define which cases always stop for human review, which can be approved in batches and how the person receives the underlying evidence. A high-stakes invoice, disputed warranty or sensitive contact record warrants a more cautious path than a low-risk internal tag.

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework provides a useful map of governance and evaluation questions. It is not vendor validation and will not replace a tested operating runbook.

The economics of the year after launch.

Separate upfront implementation fees from model usage, monitoring software, vendor subscriptions, engineering support and human time spent correcting mistakes. A cheap build with no support may be more expensive than a smaller supported workflow. Ask which events trigger additional project fees, what happens when the model provider changes pricing and whether the data can be migrated to another implementation.

The ideal provider can explain an error budget and service boundaries in ordinary business language. Larsen's explicit maintenance offering is a reason to ask for those details, not proof they will always be handled for free.

A ninety-day operating review.

After launch, review the first 30, 60 and 90 days: valid task completion, exception volume, rollback events, latency, costs and changes to the underlying business process. Keep a rollback option until the new workflow has earned trust. The real achievement of automation is not the number of AI decisions made, but how much correct work the team can rely on.
