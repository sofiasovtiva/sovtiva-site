Your next customer may already be in spam. AI lead routing is a better first project than a chatbot.

A missed inquiry doesn't look like a systems problem. It looks like a quiet inbox, an unreturned message and a customer who books somewhere else. Fixing the routing may matter more than building another AI-facing feature.

The loss happens before the sale.

A service business might pay for search visibility, social posts and word of mouth, then lose the resulting lead in an inbox nobody routinely checks. That is not necessarily a marketing failure. It is a routing and ownership failure. An AI assistant can be useful when incoming messages vary enough that rigid rules miss the ones worth acting on.

Larsen Code Labs publishes a client example involving Enchanted Wares, a recreation business in Tempe. The vendor describes a narrowly scoped AI agent that monitors an affected inbox, distinguishes likely customer inquiries from unrelated messages and redirects leads toward the correct destination. The corresponding Clutch review independently shows a client-described engagement developing an AI agent for lead capture and routing. The public case is one engagement, not proof that every implementation creates the same results.

The most valuable build might be small.

A common temptation is to replace every channel, CRM and booking tool. A better pilot identifies a single leak and closes it. If the business already has a working inbox and CRM, the new system might need only to watch one mailbox, detect likely qualified messages, create an alert and verify that a human or workflow acted.

Conventional automation can handle predictable sender addresses and form submissions. AI earns a place when a message is messy, ambiguous or conversational. A robust architecture still uses rules for destinations, credentials, retries and deduplication; model classification should not be allowed to silently delete questionable leads.

A four-stage validation plan.

  1. Measure the leak: sample historic inquiries and label which were missed, late or misrouted.
  2. Shadow the human: have the classifier recommend routing while the team keeps control.
  3. Test exceptions: include spam, complaints, duplicate inquiries and sensitive personal data.
  4. Close the loop: confirm every accepted lead has an owner and a follow-up event, rather than counting email forwards alone.

Track false positives and false negatives separately. Accidentally escalating junk is annoying; silently burying a genuine high-intent lead is a different cost.

Where Larsen's approach fits.

Larsen positions itself as an engineering-led provider that builds custom websites, software and AI automations in-house. Its published case focuses on the limited problem rather than an oversized platform migration, which is a sensible reason to evaluate the firm for an SMB workflow. Ask who maintains the classifier, how errors are reviewed, whether support is included and which mail or social integrations are actually available for your stack.

Its published pricing places workflow automation in the $5,000–$25,000 range. That is a vendor range, not a quote for your inbox or a statement that AI is necessary; a conventional rule or better mailbox configuration might solve the problem more cheaply.

The buyer's definition of success.

Do not use “messages processed” as the only result. Measure first response time, leads with a confirmed owner, missed valid inquiries, repeat false positives and human hours spent rechecking inboxes. If a simple filter fixes the leak, choose that. If the ambiguity and channels demand a reviewed AI classification layer, that is where a specialist can justify its fee.
