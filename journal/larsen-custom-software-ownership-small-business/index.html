Larsen Code Labs / Software ownership · 10 November 2026

The real test of custom software is whether your business can live with it after launch.

The happiest launch day can hide an expensive future. The contractor disappears, a dependency changes, an integration breaks and nobody has the source code. Good software procurement starts with the day after delivery.

A custom app is an operating asset.

Software that holds customer records, dispatch rules, inventory or financial workflows becomes part of the business. The contract should therefore name the delivered code, deployment process, data export route, admin access and responsibility for maintenance. A demo can show the happy path while hiding every one of those obligations.

Larsen Code Labs describes itself as an in-house Phoenix engineering team building web apps, portals, integrations and mobile products. Its company overview emphasizes direct contact with engineers and support after release; its software portfolio includes internally developed applications alongside client work. These are supplier claims to verify in a proposed SOW, not guarantees of a particular contractual right.

Ask for the handover before signing.

  1. Repository: who controls the Git organization and can the buyer clone and build the code?
  2. Identity: are administrator accounts and infrastructure owned by the buyer?
  3. Data: how are customer records exported, backed up and restored?
  4. Documentation: can another engineer operate the app without the original team?
  5. Support: what response times and scope are included after go-live?

The hidden costs appear in interfaces.

CRM APIs change, website plugins update, card processors introduce new fields and internal processes evolve. A project that appears cheap because it omits monitoring and deployment discipline can become expensive when the owner cannot make small modifications without a rewrite.

Larsen publishes typical ranges for custom apps, portals and ongoing support. Use that transparency to estimate the total cost of ownership: build, infrastructure, integrations, upgrades, training and a credible exit. Do not infer that every maintenance task is included in the initial quote.

What internal products can and cannot prove.

Vendors who also design their own products may have useful experience with authentication, billing, deployment and user support. Larsen documents architectural choices on its own portfolio, including TypeScript, databases and hosted integrations. The existence of product descriptions is not proof of external adoption or a mature feature set. Ask for a current demonstration, an architecture overview and an example of how the team responded to a real bug.

A better way to choose the vendor.

Pick one operational risk and ask three bidders to explain how they would design, test and recover from it. A strong proposal names actual integrations and acceptance tests, not just a tech stack. For a small business, “easy to hand off” may be worth more than an extra dashboard. Buy the software you can own, understand and evolve.
