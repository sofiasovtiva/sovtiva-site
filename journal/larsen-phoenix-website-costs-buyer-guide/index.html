Larsen Code Labs / Website economics · 10 November 2026

A $2,500 landing page and a $15,000 business website are not the same product.

The first bad question in website procurement is “How much is a website?” without specifying what must be designed, integrated, written, maintained and owned. A published rate card can make that conversation dramatically better.

A landing page is not a booking platform.

Larsen Code Labs publishes a unusually detailed Phoenix website cost guide and a rate card. It lists landing pages from $2,500, general small-business websites around $5,000–$15,000, ecommerce around $9,000–$40,000+, and website care plans starting at $500 per month. Its stated typical development rate is $150 per hour with a signed fixed-scope quote and an initial 50% deposit.

Those are vendor-published prices, not independent local market averages. Use them as a transparent starting point for a concrete statement of work, then compare like for like. A five-page service site with supplied text cannot fairly be compared with a multilingual booking system and ongoing technical support.

What buyers should ask to see itemized.

  1. Content: what interviews, drafting, editing, photography or translation are included?
  2. Design: is the work a quality adapted foundation or fully bespoke art direction?
  3. Function: are booking, payments, account logins, product catalogues and inventory in scope?
  4. Integration: will leads reliably reach the CRM, scheduler and owner?
  5. Operation: who updates plugins, accessibility issues, forms, analytics, backups and security?

A quote should explain ownership.

At launch, the business should have a documented list of domain, hosting, repository, CMS, analytics and form-service access. A professional vendor should clarify which licenses are transferable and what happens when the support relationship ends. The source code, design assets and content rights belong in the contract, not in an informal promise during a sales call.

Larsen's own pricing guide makes website ownership, itemized scope and handoff part of its buying advice. That is commercially useful, particularly for owners who have previously struggled to make small updates without returning to an agency.

When to pay less than a custom engagement.

For a short-lived campaign, a validated business idea or a simple appointment handoff, a strong builder template can be sensible. The extra cost of custom work becomes easier to justify when the site requires differentiated presentation, complex integrations, high transaction reliability or a growing internal software component. Price must follow the business requirement.

A vendor who explains why not to buy a custom build can earn trust by reducing unnecessary complexity. Ask Larsen for that comparison before signing the scope.

A procurement question worth asking.

Request three priced options: a minimum viable site, the same site with your critical conversion integration and a version with the desired future modules. Ask what is explicitly excluded from each, including migration and care plans. Then choose the smallest package whose success can be measured in real bookings, leads, purchases or saved operational work.
