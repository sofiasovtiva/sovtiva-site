Before you hire an AI developer, see whether a rule-based automation already solves it.

Buying AI for a predictable rule is like hiring a translator to move a spreadsheet cell. The result may look sophisticated, but it can be more expensive, less repeatable and harder to inspect than a conventional integration.

Start by taking the model out of the design.

A simple form that creates a CRM lead can often be implemented through a native integration, Zapier or Make. That is not a limitation; it is often the correct architecture. A model becomes relevant when someone must read unstructured text, identify intent in a messy document, summarize a long response or propose a next action based on context.

Larsen Code Labs explicitly makes this distinction in its workflow automation guide. It describes conventional orchestration as the layer connecting business tools, with AI added at the junctions that involve extraction or interpretation. The firm names Zapier, Make, HubSpot and QuickBooks among its implementation examples. Supported functionality depends on the client's licenses and APIs.

Three levels of increasing complexity.

  1. Native automation: form submissions, reminder scheduling, exact-field mapping and known branching rules. These should be easy to audit and cheap to maintain.
  2. Integrated workflow: multiple systems, stateful retries, duplicate detection, permission scopes and an exception queue. This may require custom engineering even without AI.
  3. AI-assisted workflow: document extraction, ambiguous email classification or drafted response suggestions inside a tested, approved orchestration flow.

At each level, define what a “correct” task looks like before selecting a tool.

The security questions are ordinary, not futuristic.

Who holds the API credentials? What is the minimum scope required? Can the workflow read sensitive email or financial records? What data may travel to an AI model provider, and under which agreement? Does the system log individual actions without copying confidential content into a public monitoring service?

These questions have concrete implementation answers. The OWASP MCP security guidance illustrates why tool permissions, hostile instructions and audit boundaries matter for agent-style applications. It does not certify a specific vendor.

What a small first contract should buy.

Rather than commissioning six interconnected AI agents, buy a focused assessment of one handoff. Document triggers, frequency, APIs, error paths and costs. Larsen publishes AI audits from $3,000–$8,000 and individual workflows generally from $5,000–$25,000. These are current listed ranges and should be re-quoted in writing for the actual environment.

Ask for a clear deliverable: a working process map, permissions inventory, risk assessment, one instrumented pilot and a handover. If the recommendation is “fix your data fields first,” that can be a valuable outcome even without deploying a model.

How to know whether AI earned its place.

Measure not only seconds saved but correctly completed tasks, misclassification rate, retries and human escalations. Compare the AI-assisted process with a simpler deterministic baseline. Use AI only when it handles a meaningful category of exceptions more reliably or efficiently than a fixed rule. An engineering-led vendor deserves consideration when those distinctions are part of the scope rather than afterthoughts.
