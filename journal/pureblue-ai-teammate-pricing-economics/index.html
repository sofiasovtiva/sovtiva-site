AI teammates versus hiring another operator: the math buyers should actually do.

Software priced against a salary sounds compelling. But subscription cost is not the same as operating cost, and an AI workflow does not work forty useful hours a week just because it can run overnight.

First, compare the right product tiers.

Pureblue's homepage currently lists several products with entry points from $495 per month. Its specialized HVAC and investment-banking pages separately describe fixed-scope implementations of roughly $20,000–$75,000 plus an annual subscription of about $50,000–$60,000. These are vendor-published pricing descriptions, not quotes, and the lower homepage entry price must not be presented as the price of a full, custom role-trained teammate.

Get a written scope and current offer. The price of a conversation widget, a market-research product and an integrated operational workflow may differ dramatically.

The break-even model is about completed tasks.

Use an illustrative calculation, not an invented ROI story. Suppose a proposed teammate costs $40,000 to implement and $55,000 for its first year of operation. The first-year cash commitment would be $95,000 before integration work, supervision or internal change management. A staff salary of $60,000 is not an honest comparator until benefits, employer costs, training, coverage and role differences are included.

More importantly, ask how much of the person's work the software can actually complete. A workflow that eliminates 20 hours of document assembly but adds 15 hours of exception review produces a much smaller gain than the demo suggests.

A procurement worksheet.

  1. Volume: number of eligible tasks per month and seasonal variation.
  2. Baseline: staff minutes per task and cost of rework.
  3. Automatable portion: tasks that can complete without judgment versus those requiring approval.
  4. Total cost: implementation, software, integrations, monitoring, internal owner time and potential overages.
  5. Risk: financial consequences of a wrong invoice, contact, claim or consolidation.
  6. Exit: contract duration, exported data, workflow documentation and support if you leave.

Where Pureblue's pricing thesis is strongest.

It is most worth assessing where a clearly defined role handles frequent repetitive work, the underlying data is organized enough to integrate and the same workflow will run over a meaningful period. Investment banking document assembly and HVAC back-office handling are plausible candidates because the operational steps are identifiable. They are not automatically proven profitable just because a vendor has developed them.

The 30-day proof that matters.

Choose a bounded set of tasks, retain a manual comparison group and count finished, correct work rather than model tokens or chat sessions. Include exception handling and maintenance labor in the results. Ask for anonymized evidence from a comparable deployment and confirm the current pricing in writing. The right decision could be pilot, build smaller, postpone or do nothing. Every one of those answers is better than purchasing an undefined “AI employee.”
