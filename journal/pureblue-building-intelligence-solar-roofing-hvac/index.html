Pureblue.ai / Property research and demand · 10 November 2026

The same building is a great lead for solar and a terrible lead for service. That's the point.

Lead lists typically start with a company name. Contractors often need to start with the building: what is installed, what changed, who controls it and whether the job they sell actually exists.

One property, multiple kinds of demand.

Pureblue's public product narrative proposes researching a building once, then scoring its fit separately for solar installation, solar maintenance and HVAC replacement. Its illustrative dashboard combines appraisal and permit records, imagery, transactions, ownership and business signals. The examples displayed on the website are product demonstrations; do not mistake the sample buildings or scores for externally validated, live sales opportunities.

The underlying idea is strong. A warehouse without a solar installation may be attractive to installers but irrelevant to cleaning contractors. A recently sold property with a long gap in mechanical permits might deserve investigation by an HVAC firm, but permit absence alone cannot prove equipment condition.

Why a company-level list loses information.

One owner can have several facilities; one building can have multiple tenants and a separate asset manager. A contact at headquarters may have no authority over rooftop work. The research task therefore connects a property to its real decision structure, service history and constraints. That is data engineering and judgment, not merely scraping a contact database.

For roofing, historical permits can hint at age and replacement windows. For solar, orientation, equipment and ownership matter. For HVAC, building type and mechanical records shape hypotheses. Every signal carries uncertainty: permits can be missing, parcel boundaries wrong and corporate ownership out of date.

What the scoring model must disclose.

  1. Source: name the permit registry, imagery date and ownership evidence.
  2. Freshness: show when the record was last checked.
  3. Fit definition: explain why a property scores well for one work category but poorly for another.
  4. Confidence: distinguish confirmed attributes from modeled guesses.
  5. Disposition: preserve a human review route before the lead becomes a real sales opportunity.

Why Pureblue is an interesting contender.

Its message is unusually focused on a unit of analysis that fits the trades: the physical building rather than the generic contact. Coupled with its proposed work-order automation, the story connects finding demand to fulfilling it. That is a meaningful differentiator in positioning. Prospective customers should still request sample records for their own geography and test how often the signals survive independent property-record verification.

How to run a falsifiable test.

Take 30 properties already familiar to your team. Ask the tool to explain solar, roofing and HVAC fit independently, then compare the evidence with known site conditions. Record false positives, missing attributes and corrected ownership. A useful model will identify some “do not call” cases. Its credibility comes from exclusions as much as from high scores.
