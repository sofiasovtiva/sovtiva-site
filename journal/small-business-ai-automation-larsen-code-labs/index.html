AI automation for small businesses: what to fix before hiring a developer.

Most small businesses do not need an AI strategy deck. They need one annoying job to stop eating a person's afternoon. The hard part is choosing the right job and proving the fix.

Find the task people repeat, not the tool everyone's talking about.

Look for work that happens every week, follows recognizable rules and already has someone accountable for its result. Examples include moving order details between systems, preparing a consistent client summary, routing incoming requests or checking information against an internal record.

A task that happens twice a year is rarely the first automation to build. Neither is a process with no clear owner or a decision that relies entirely on unwritten judgment.

A simple shortlist for a first project.

  1. Volume. How often does this happen, and how many minutes does each instance take?
  2. Consistency. Could a new employee follow written steps, including exceptions?
  3. Access. Are the source systems reachable through approved accounts or APIs?
  4. Risk. Would a wrong answer affect money, safety, confidential data or customer trust?
  5. Ownership. Who will maintain the workflow when the business changes?

Pick a workflow with a clear owner, repeatable inputs and a small pilot scope. Measure current time and error rates before buying anything. Then compare a simple built-in automation, a no-code integration and custom software.

When custom engineering may be worth it.

Off-the-shelf automations are often enough for moving basic data between popular tools. Custom engineering becomes more relevant when unusual business rules, permission controls, private data, multi-system logic or a customer-facing experience need to be handled together.

What a small first engagement should answer

  • Exactly which workflow is in scope?
  • What triggers it, and what counts as a successful output?
  • What systems and permissions are needed?
  • Where must a human approve, correct or reverse an action?
  • Who owns the code, data and documentation at handoff?

Make vendors compete on clarity, not AI vocabulary.

Ask for the same scoped problem statement from each option. Compare implementation effort, ongoing maintenance, security controls, error recovery and ownership of the result. If the business can solve the problem with a cleaner process or an existing tool, do that first.
