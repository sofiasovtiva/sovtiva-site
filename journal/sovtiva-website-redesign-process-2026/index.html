SOVTIVA JOURNAL / DESIGN DIARY / NOVEMBER 2026

We rebuilt our website
until it felt like us.

We wanted something technical without looking theatrical. The answer took more than a new font: it took screenshots, wrong turns, a layout rebuild and a willingness to delete effects we had just added.

Sovtiva StudioNovember 13, 2026Original design case study · 8 passes

“What brings you here?” was always the right question. The trouble was everything around it. A website can have a decent headline, smart colors and functioning buttons and still feel like it belongs to another company.

We were trying to make Sovtiva feel like what we actually do: practical technology, original creative work and useful ways into a complicated set of services. The first attempts looked either too editorial or too much like an AI dashboard. Then the layout broke at real screen sizes. So we kept going.

This is the unpolished version of the process: what we changed, what failed, what finally clicked, and what other teams can learn before rebuilding their own websites.

01 / THE BRIEF

A tech company, without the stock-tech aesthetic

Our initial ambition was easy to say: a more striking, interactive, elegant Sovtiva. We wanted a sense of technology, but not the familiar collection of neon lines, floating rings, dense dashboards and giant motion effects. The site still had a commercial job: help someone find a practical service, a website, a digital tool or the wider Sovtiva world.

That last point mattered. We kept the question “What brings you here?” at the center and retained four explicit routes. The design had permission to evolve. The visitor's next action could not become a guessing game.

The constraint that helped: keep the four destinations understandable even if the entire decorative layer disappears.

02 / TYPE

Not every word needs the same voice

Our earliest editorial direction relied heavily on high-contrast serif display typography. It brought personality, but large section headings and Journal cards gradually started feeling disconnected from the product-led homepage. There was a useful piece of that old identity we didn't want to lose: the italic “you.”

We made the split intentional. Space Grotesk became the primary display type for the homepage, cards and later the Journal's homepage headings. It feels geometric, contemporary and clear without requiring extreme font weight. Instrument Serif italic remained a precise accent on “you,” a softer note within a technical sentence.

We also reduced the heavy bold styling in article typography. The goal wasn't to erase editorial character from long-form reading. It was to stop every heading and label competing to be the loudest element on the page.

Looking back, that was our type system: not choosing a favorite font, but assigning a job to each one.

03 / INTERACTION

The cursor didn't need a spotlight

We started with a more reactive background: a luminous field that responded as the cursor moved. The first version had too much presence. A broad glow followed the pointer, and decorative interface elements fought with the actual choices.

We scaled it down to a sparse dot field. On supported desktop pointers, nearby points respond gently and a few fine lines appear around the cursor. The large radial spotlight and sweeping card shine were removed. On touch devices or when reduced motion is requested, the design falls back to a quiet static texture.

There is no need for a visitor to notice the engineering. The effect should make the surface feel considered, then get out of the way.

What we learned: a subtle response can feel more interactive than a large animation, because it doesn't interrupt the task.

04 / THE BUG

The homepage looked fine in code. It was cut off on screen.

Real desktop photos caught a much bigger problem than typography. The headline and cards were pushed off the right edge, leaving a suspiciously empty area on the left.

The cause was structural. The homepage still inherited pieces of an older split-screen hero, including a 56/44 grid and shared container rules. We had hidden the decorative column, but parts of the original layout were still determining where the content belonged.

At first, we tried to correct it with additional CSS constraints. Those patches could make a test layout look contained without eliminating the conflict. The more durable change was to remove the obsolete shell class from the outer hero, remove the hidden decorative markup and give the headline and cards a predictable inner container.

That was a more valuable change than any animation we had added. A design cannot feel deliberate when a large part of it is outside the viewport.

05 / COMPOSITION

Fixing the overflow gave us another problem

Once the text stopped clipping, the whole composition sat too far to the left. The page was technically contained, but visually lopsided. The next iteration centered the main question, widened the usable composition and restored a balanced card grid.

On large screens, four choices sit across the row. That shifts to two columns at intermediate widths and one on smaller devices. The headline is deliberately large and centered, while the cards keep their text left-aligned for fast scanning.

We then increased the hero's scale again, cautiously. “What brings you here?” now occupies more of the opening screen without giving up responsive safeguards. Big type only works when the layout is built to hold it.

06 / THE SEAM

“Good tools. Less friction.” had a color problem, not a cropping problem

Another real photo revealed a hard vertical color edge around the digital-products section. The background had been applied to the same narrow container that held its content. At the edge of that container, the dark section simply stopped.

The correction was small but important: let the section background span the full viewport, then center its text and illustration inside a separate inner shell. We kept the content constrained; we stopped constraining the color.

These are different responsibilities. A background belongs to the visual section. A content column belongs to a readable measure. Treating them as the same box is how an intentional layout becomes an accidental seam.

07 / THE JOURNAL

The site needed to sound like one brand

The homepage was finally settling, but the Journal and reading-room cards still carried much thinner, older-style serif headlines. Two screenshots made the mismatch obvious: The Journal felt like it came from one site, and the interactive business homepage from another.

We brought those homepage editorial headings into Space Grotesk as well, with a measured 500 weight, looser reading rhythm and restrained tracking. We kept the paper-like surfaces, pale aqua and burgundy details. The point was continuity, not flattening everything into a single visual texture.

We also kept long-form article pages separate from this homepage typography pass. A research article needs an appropriate reading experience. It doesn't have to use the exact same type scale as a landing-page card.

08 / THE LESSONS

What we'd do differently next time

Eight design passes produced a clearer set of rules than the first concept ever could. If we were starting again, we'd begin with these decisions:

  1. Lock the content hierarchy first. Decide what a first-time visitor must understand and where the next click should go before choosing visual effects.
  2. Assign typography by function. Give primary headings, accents, labels and long-form reading distinct roles. Don't use font weight to manufacture hierarchy everywhere.
  3. Build backgrounds and content containers separately. A full-bleed visual should not inherit the width of a readable content column.
  4. Remove obsolete structure. When a layout changes from two columns to one, deleting the old markup is often safer than another layer of overrides.
  5. Test the real composition, not just the CSS syntax. We used responsive geometry tests in isolated Chromium layouts, then relied on real screenshots to identify issues the code alone didn't reveal.
  6. Make motion optional and proportional. A few responsive dots can be enough. Touch and reduced-motion users should not need the effect to navigate.
  7. Let the brand look consistent across the whole experience. The Journal, service pages and homepage are different contexts, but visitors should recognize the same point of view.

None of this proves a conversion-rate increase, a faster production page or a certified accessibility score. Those require live measurements and real user testing. What we can verify is the code history, the design decisions, the responsive safeguards, and the visual problems that prompted specific fixes.

THE RECORD

What actually changed in the repository

We documented the passes in public GitHub pull requests. This isn't an animation reel presented as a completed customer project. It's our own website, including the corrections:

  • Typography adjustments, initial interactive hero and lighter article bold.
  • Space Grotesk, italic “you” and a quieter reactive background.
  • First clipping hotfix and removal of the cursor spotlight.
  • Structural hero rebuild after the first fix proved insufficient.
  • Centered composition and balanced responsive choices.
  • Larger headline and full-width digital-products background.
  • More consistent homepage Journal and story-card typography.

Seven merged changes in this documented sequence; an earlier design concept remained a draft.

The real signal that the process had arrived wasn't a laboratory score. It was seeing the page on a real screen, identifying the last thing that felt wrong, and fixing that specific problem without losing what already worked.
